Twenty-six years of California swell

The whole California coast as a fringe. Every strand is one of CDIP's 11,594 alongshore MOP sites, streaming inshore along the direction its wave energy actually arrives from — swell and sea drawn as two independent layers, because for most of the record they are two different waves going two different ways.
Direction here is the band-split energy flux, not the mean direction. Sea and swell each carry 30–70% of the flux about two thirds of the time, and their flux directions differ by 22° on average — so a single mean direction is perfectly smooth and describes neither of them.

Data. CDIP MOP alongshore stations — 11,594 sites across all 15 California coastal counties at ~107 m median spacing. The record is continuous and hourly from 2000-01-01 to the present: _hindcast runs to 2025-03-31 23:00 and the alongshore _nowcast picks up at 2025-04-01 with no gap. (That switchover is the one in read_MOPline2.m; note the alongshore nowcast is a rolling multi-year archive, unlike the gridded nowcast, which is only a six-hour buffer.) Years are sampled daily; events are hourly.

Method. For each frequency band the wave energy flux is ρg·Cg·a1·E and ρg·Cg·b1·E summed over the band, following estimateEnergyFlux in read_MOPline2.m, using that routine's canonical bands — swell 0.02–0.0813 Hz, sea 0.09–0.400 Hz. Group speed comes from the linear dispersion relation at each site's own depth. Reproducing the total-band flux direction matches the file's own waveDm to a 1.80° mean offset with 1.38° residual; the small remaining difference is the Cg weighting, which waveDm does not carry. Strand length scales with √Hs, and the two layers advance at different rates because swell group speed is roughly twice that of local sea.

The yearly view is a snapshot, not a daily summary. Years are sampled by taking every 24th hourly step, so each frame is the state at 00:00 UTC — not a daily mean and not a daily maximum. Short-lived peaks are therefore attenuated: the December 2015 Humboldt event reads 13.5 m in the yearly view where the hourly event view reaches 14.8 m. Use an event for the peak of a storm; use a year to see the season.

Events are derived, not remembered. They are chosen by ranking every day in the record on the statewide 90th-percentile swell-band Hs, so an event has to be large along a broad stretch of coast rather than at one exposed site, with a minimum separation so a single storm cannot fill the list. Because that ranking runs on the daily snapshots, a storm peaking between samples can rank below its true severity — the result is a defensible shortlist, not a definitive ordering of the record.

The Hs color and length scale is per payload. Each file carries its own hsMax, measured from its own peak, because no fixed ceiling survived contact with the record — 8 m clipped January 2023 and 12 m still clipped December 2015. Physical values in the readout are therefore always correct, but strand length is not comparable between a calm year and a stormy one without reading the scale.

Coverage is not uniform across the record. MOP's band estimates depend on which offshore buoys were feeding the model at the time, and that changed: Del Norte has no sea-band estimate at all until October 2005, Humboldt carries one in about 84% of months, Santa Barbara in 100%. Those gaps arrive as exactly zero energy, which once drawn is indistinguishable from flat calm — so the panel reports the fraction of sites actually carrying an estimate for the frame on screen, and missing sites are left blank rather than drawn at zero.

What this is not. This is a chain of points, not a field: unlike the live page, there is no 2-D grid to integrate through, so each strand is the straight local ray at its own site rather than a refracting path. Strand pacing is scaled for legibility, not real time. Every site sits at its own model depth (10 m for most), so this is a nearshore quantity, not a deep-water one.
