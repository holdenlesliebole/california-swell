The California swell field

Every strand is a wave ray — a path traced through the peak-direction field of CDIP's MOP nearshore wave model, running live off the Scripps THREDDS server. Strand speed is the group velocity, solved cell by cell from the linear dispersion relation, so strands slow and crowd as they climb the shelf. Zoom anywhere on the coast and the grid sharpens from ~2 km to the county products at ~100–200 m.
Where the strands converge, you are seeing refractive focusing — the same process that decides which stretch of beach takes the energy out of a given swell. The pattern is the physics, not the rendering.

Data. CDIP MOP gridded nowcast + forecast. CA_0.01 covers the state at ~1 km and is shipped decimated 2× to ~2 km, which is ample at that zoom; all fifteen county grids are available beneath it — DN, HU, M, SN, MA, SF, SM, SC, MO, SL at ~200 m and B, VE, L, OC, D at ~100 m — so the coast is covered continuously from the Mexican border to Oregon. Only the county you are looking at is fetched, one at a time. The window is rolling and runs from about six hours behind the present to nearly five days ahead. Its cadence is not uniform — the nowcast is hourly, then the forecast steps every six hours — so playback advances at a constant rate in simulated time rather than a constant rate in frames, and the forecast does not race past. The badge reads nowcast or forecast either side of the present, and turns red with an age if the scheduled rebuild has stopped and the data is no longer current.

Method. Strand direction is the local peak direction Dp, so in the geometric-optics limit the strands are wave rays. Speed is the group velocity from the linear dispersion relation, with the wavenumber from Guo's (2002) explicit fit (~0.75% error) — no iteration, which keeps it cheap enough to run per particle per frame. Time is interpolated between hourly fields on the vector components rather than the angle, so there is no artifact at the 0/360° seam. Coastline and the model-domain edge are separated by depth at the mask boundary, which a flood fill cannot do: the MOP domains are staircases of nested boxes, and at every step corner the offshore padding abuts the continent directly.

Strands fade out across bimodal fronts. Dp is a peak statistic, so where a decaying swell and a rising wind sea carry comparable energy, the spectral peak flips between the two systems from one cell to the next and Dp jumps tens of degrees across a sharp front — cells either side differ in Tp by about 6 s, against 0.05 s elsewhere, which is the giveaway that this is two wave systems trading places rather than noise. Smoothing Dp would be worse than the disease: the average of two distinct wave systems is a direction in which no wave is travelling. Instead the local directional coherence is measured and the field is allowed to go quiet where a single direction stops describing the sea. On a bimodal frame that touches roughly 5–8% of cells and on a clean one about 1%; smooth refraction gradients score at the top of the scale and are drawn in full.

What this is not. Strands are rays, not water-particle trajectories and not the orbital motion under a wave — nothing here is advecting a physical parcel. Ray convergence indicates focusing, but this is a visualization, not a ray solver: it traces an already-computed Dp field rather than integrating the eikonal equation, so caustics are implied, not resolved. Strand pacing is scaled for legibility, not real time. Color ranges are percentile-clipped per build, so a calm summer day and a winter event are not on a common scale.

Sea and swell come from a different product. The gridded files publish bulk Hs/Tp/Dp/Ta only — their waveFrequency dimension carries no data variable, so there is no per-frequency energy and no a1/b1 with which to partition a spectrum. A band split is therefore impossible from the grid, at any resolution. The alongshore MOP stations do carry spectra, so the split is computed there — swell 0.02–0.0813 Hz, sea 0.09–0.400 Hz, direction from the band energy flux — and drawn as a coastal fringe over the field. Because the two are genuinely different products, the fringe is drawn as discrete vectors rather than blended into the fur, and it updates every three hours rather than hourly, which is the alongshore forecast's own cadence.

Build. THREDDS sends no CORS header, so the page cannot read OPeNDAP directly. A build step (build/build_data.py) pulls the grids, merges nowcast with forecast, and quantizes Hs/Tp/Dp to one byte over a wet-cell mask — 0.5 MB for the state, 1.4 MB for San Diego, the latter fetched only when you zoom in. Renderer is plain JavaScript, no dependencies, no build step.
